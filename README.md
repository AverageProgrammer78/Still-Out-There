# Still Out There

**An interactive 3D flight tour of the NASA robots left across the solar system, told in their own voices.**

Made for the NASA Space Apps Challenge, "Tell the Story of NASA Hardware Left Across the Solar System."
Written for ages 10 to 14.

## The idea

Kids usually hear about active missions. They rarely hear about the rovers, landers and probes that finished their jobs and are still out there. *Still Out There* lets you fly across a 3D solar system, visit 17 of these machines, and hear each one tell its own story. A glowing coral beacon marks the 3 machines that are still talking to Earth, and an ice-blue beacon marks the 14 that have gone quiet.

## What you can do

- **Fly the tour.** Press "Begin at the Moon" and follow the machines from the Moon, to Mars, to orbit around the Sun, to the outer solar system.
- **See the distance.** Moving between regions flies the camera along a curved transfer arc from the departure planet to the arrival planet, so you feel how far apart worlds are. Moving within a region is a short local glide.
- **Hear each robot.** Every stop has a first-person quote, "the science it made possible," and a link to the NASA source.
- **Live telemetry.** Each stop shows distance from Earth and one-way radio time (for example "1.3 seconds" for the Moon, about 11 minutes for Mars today, nearly 24 hours for Voyager 1).
- **Send signal to Earth.** Watch a light pulse travel from the spacecraft back to Earth.
- **Look back at Earth.** Turn the camera to see how small home looks from that robot's point of view.
- **Explore freely.** Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom, and grab the screen mid-flight to take over.
- **Sound (optional).** A synthesized telemetry chime and thruster whoosh. It is muted until you click "Sound: Off".

## Data and sources

Mission facts, dates and statuses come from NASA mission pages and the NASA Space Science Data Coordinated Archive (NSSDCA). Every stop links to its source.

- Mission pages: `science.nasa.gov/mission/...`
- NSSDCA: `nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov`
- **Mars distance and light time** are calculated live from planetary positions, using the JPL "Approximate Positions of the Planets" orbital elements.
- **Voyager 1 distance** uses NASA's published milestone of one light-day (about 25.9 billion km) on Nov 18, 2026, extended at its current rate.
- **New Horizons, Pioneer 10 and Kepler** distances are estimates from published speeds and orbits, labeled "about" on the page.
- **NEAR and Dawn** show distance ranges, since asteroid distances change a lot.

## Accuracy notes

- The solar system map is a stylized drawing, **not to scale**.
- The planets, galaxies and 3D spacecraft are **simplified illustrations generated by the page**, not photographs or exact engineering models. Several machines share a similar model (for example Phoenix and InSight).
- Statuses ("still talking" or "gone quiet") reflect NASA information at the time of writing and may change.

## How it is built

- One file: `index.html`. No build step and no audio or image files.
- [Three.js](https://threejs.org/) r128 (loaded from cdnjs) for the 3D scene, with custom GLSL shaders for the nebula, stars and flight "woosh" streaks.
- Procedurally generated planet textures and nebula.
- Custom damped orbit controls and transfer-orbit camera flights.
- Web Audio API for all sound.
- Fonts: Young Serif and Lexend (Google Fonts).

## Run it

Open `index.html` in a modern browser (Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari with WebGL). It needs an internet connection for Three.js and the fonts.

**Host it on GitHub Pages:**
1. Put `index.html` and this README in a GitHub repo.
2. Go to Settings, then Pages, and choose the main branch.
3. Open the Pages link and test it in a private window.

## Accessibility and fallbacks

- Respects "reduce motion": flights jump instead of animating, and star twinkle is off.
- Keyboard controls for the whole tour.
- If WebGL is unavailable, the story text and controls still work.
- Sound is off by default.

## Known limitations

- The heavy 3D scene may run slowly on older phones or laptops.
- Planets load in over a few seconds when the page first opens.
- Telemetry for estimated machines is approximate.
